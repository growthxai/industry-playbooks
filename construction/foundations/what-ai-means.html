What AI actually means for your construction firm in 2026

Matthew Panzarino

March 2026

Your team keeps hearing about AI from vendors, trade shows, and that one PM who watched a webinar. But you still need a straight answer about whether any of this will save time or protect margin. You can't afford to chase hype. You also can't afford to ignore features that might already be bundled into software you're paying for.

Here's what the surveys actually say. Most of the construction industry hasn't started. The firms that have are seeing real results on specific workflows. And the right first move costs less than what the conference speakers implied.

You are not behind. The data proves it.

Forty-five percent of AGC member firms have not initiated any AI use in the past year. That's the single largest response category in the national contractor survey of 857 contractor members. "No AI use" beat every specific application listed.

The global picture is similar. The global industry research, based on over 2,200 built-environment professionals, found that nearly three in four organizations are either exploring AI in a limited way or have no plans at all. Only 1.5% reported AI use across multiple processes. Fewer than 1% have anything resembling organization-wide deployment.

Meanwhile, 82% of AEC respondents expect AI to drive major industry change, according to ENR's reporting on industry readiness data. But only about 20% describe their firms as ready for it.

That gap between "this matters" and "we've done something about it" is probably where you sit. You still have room to start smart instead of scrambling. And most firms getting results are working in office and preconstruction, not in futuristic jobsite use cases.

Where your peers are using AI today

Most firms using AI today are using it in preconstruction and office workflows. The jobsite stuff gets the headlines, but it's a fraction of actual adoption.

The AGC survey data shows where the activity is concentrated.

AI use case% of firms using it
Design or preconstruction39%
Estimating23%
Scheduling20%
Procurement16%
Onsite monitoring or documentation7%

Onsite adoption sits at 7%. The real activity is in preconstruction and estimating, where the math on time savings is easiest to justify.

Estimating and takeoff

Estimating is where software saves the most obvious time because it cuts manual quantity calculations. Estimators spend most of a bid cycle on quantities rather than analysis, counting and organizing what your team would otherwise do by hand. AI-assisted takeoff platforms read digital drawings, automate those calculations, and some now pull live material pricing. ServiceTitan's Atlas AI, per ENR, introduced spec-based estimating that feeds directly into procurement systems. On thin margins, the difference between a 2023 material price and a 2026 price can swing a bid from profitable to a loss.

Document analysis and RFI work

Document review targets some of the most tedious hours in a PE's week.

Project engineers often spend half a day reading through spec books, sometimes 1,000+ pages, to flag non-standard requirements and figure out which items need RFIs. One workflow that's already in use has a tool process a specification section, returns a structured list of anomalies with draft RFI language, and can review multiple sections into one consolidated report. What used to be a half-day grind becomes a short review and editing pass.

Progress tracking

Progress tracking still lags behind office use cases in adoption, but the firms doing it are posting hard numbers.

Hensel Phelps deployed an AI-powered progress-tracking platform across over 200 jobsites. The company reported its automated tracking has documented savings.

Put that in margin terms. On a project running 3% net, that savings equals the profit from roughly $11 million in additional revenue. Without winning a single new contract.

One real example, start to finish

Zachry Construction is worth looking at because they documented the whole process publicly. On a a highway project, they deployed an AI-assisted scheduling platform. Before it, Ranjeet Gadhoke, Zachry's VP of Project Controls, spent two of three weeks in a bid cycle just building the schedule. Each scenario took a day or two to evaluate.

With the platform, Zachry's team ran roughly 60 scenarios in three minutes. They reduced flat work crews from nine to seven and saved 28 total days through resequencing and resource reallocation.

Those numbers come from Gadhoke directly, quoted in Construction Dive. Not from a vendor sales sheet. And the platform predates the current wave of AI features. This kind of value didn't suddenly appear in 2026. For most firms, the question is why it feels more urgent now.

What's actually new in 2026

The biggest shift isn't a new product category. It's that AI is getting embedded into software your team already uses, which means the barrier to trying it just dropped.

Not everything labeled "AI" is new. Some products just relabeled old features. Here's what genuinely changed.

Inside the platforms you already use

In January 2026, Procore acquired a vertical AI firm. If you're a Procore customer, those document capabilities are being folded into the system you already log into.

On the field side, site monitoring platforms can now generate written safety and progress reports rather than just flagging visual differences. AI-assisted permitting tools drew commercial funding in late 2025. Neither is as mature as the preconstruction tools yet. For most GCs, the bigger near-term win is still whatever's already embedded in your project management platform.

What is still mostly incremental

Scheduling platforms are often enhanced versions of earlier schedule-optimization systems with better interfaces bolted on. Be skeptical when a vendor relabels existing features as "AI-powered." Even with easier access, most firms hit the same operational walls before they can use these tools well.

Four barriers to AI adoption (and how to handle each)

Most construction firms don't stall because the software is weak. They stall for operational reasons.

Barrier 1: People and skills

The RICS 2025 survey found the top barriers were limited organizational capability and the need for upskilling. For a 100-person GC without a dedicated IT department, that's a real structural constraint. You're not going to solve it with a lunch-and-learn.

Barrier 2: Data readiness

Data quality barriers showed up repeatedly in the research. If your specifications and submittals are scanned PDFs rather than machine-readable documents, software can't process them without conversion first. If the document is basically a photograph of text, the AI has nothing to work with. Before selecting a pilot, have someone check whether your key project documents are machine-readable.

Barrier 3: Regulation and uncertainty

In Bluebeam's 2024 survey, reported by ASCE, 69% of AEC respondents said uncertainty around potential AI regulations had affected their implementation plans. That number is high enough to shape procurement decisions across the industry.

Barrier 4: Process discipline

If your change order process on a GMP contract is a mess without AI, it will be a faster mess with AI. The tool accelerates whatever workflow you feed it, good or broken. AGC's workforce conference documentation discusses AI in the context of operational processes and workforce strategy. Fix the process first.

The common thread is that every one of these is an operations problem, not a technology problem. You don't solve them with a strategy deck. You solve them by testing something small and learning from it.

What to do in the next 30 days

The right next step isn't a company-wide rollout. It's a contained test.

Log into your Procore or project management platform and check which AI features are already included in your subscription. Procore offers a free training course that requires no technical background. This costs you nothing but an afternoon.

Then have your project management team list every recurring admin task that eats two or more hours per week. Daily construction reports. RFI drafting and cross-referencing. Submittal log management. Safety inspection report generation. Pick the one that causes the most pain.

Take a real (non-confidential) document from a current project and run a small test. For a spec section, try a prompt like "Review this specification section and list any requirements that differ from standard practice for commercial concrete work, along with suggested RFI questions for each." You'll know in minutes whether the output is useful.

Put someone in charge. Not IT. Designate a senior PM or operations manager who bridges field and office. Construction Executive makes the case for dedicated AI leadership in construction firms. JE Dunn Construction created an internal committee run by operators, not IT. Operators identified use cases, IT received them for implementation, per industry coverage.

Run a 60-day pilot with metrics defined before you start. One workflow, one project, whether it's a lump-sum bid or a GMP job. Measure how long the task takes now. After 60 days, measure again. If you can't measure the difference, you can't justify expanding. Construction Dive recommends starting with small-scale pilots that show results before committing to broader rollout.

The bottom line

AI in construction in 2026 isn't going to change how you pour concrete or hang steel. What it does change is the office and preconstruction work that eats your team's time before and after the field work happens. Forty-five percent of your peers haven't started. The ones who have are posting real time savings on estimating, document review, progress tracking, and scheduling.

Hensel Phelps and Zachry both followed the same pattern. Pick a painful task, test a capability on one project, measure what happened, expand only after the numbers justified it.

Check what your platform already offers, test it on one real document, put an operator (not IT) in charge, and measure before and after. That's the whole move.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI replace my estimators or project engineers?

No. The AGC 2025 Workforce Survey shows only 12% of contractors expect AI will eliminate jobs, down from 17% in 2023. The more pressing problem is the retirement wave among experienced estimators. AI-assisted estimating platforms help one experienced estimator handle more work, not replace them.

How much does this cost to get started?

Your cheapest entry point is functionality already embedded in platforms you pay for. Procore's AI features and their free training course cost nothing beyond your existing subscription. A 60-day pilot on one workflow with existing platforms can produce results before you evaluate any new vendor contracts.

Is our project data good enough to start?

Probably not across the board, and that's fine. Construction Dive recommends defining data standards and moving away from unstructured, inconsistent deliverables when adopting AI. Fix data quality one document type at a time, alongside a pilot. Don't treat it as a prerequisite.

What if it doesn't work?

That's why you start with one workflow on one project with metrics defined upfront. A failed 60-day pilot on daily report generation costs minimal time and zero capital if you used existing platforms. Scope your risk to match your confidence.
